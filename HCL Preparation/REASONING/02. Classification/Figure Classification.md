# Figure Classification

## 1. Core Concept

> [!summary]
> **Figure Classification** identifies the figure that does not follow the same visual or logical rule as the other figures.

The basic idea is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Find the common visual property shared by most figures}}
$$

Then identify the figure that breaks that property.

Unlike word or number classification, the relationship may be based on:

- Shape
- Number of sides
- Number of elements
- Position
- Direction
- Rotation
- Reflection
- Shading
- Symmetry
- Size
- Number of lines
- Number of dots
- Internal arrangement
- Combination of features

### Intuition

Do not ask:

> "Which figure looks strange?"

Ask:

> **"What exact property do three or more figures have in common?"**

---

# 2. Basic Meaning

Figure classification is a **non-verbal reasoning** problem.

You are given several figures and must select the figure that belongs to a different group.

Example:

- Circle
- Triangle
- Square
- Pentagon

If the first three are classified by having fewer than five sides while the fourth has five sides, the answer is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Pentagon}}
$$

The actual intended rule depends on the complete question.

---

# 3. Main Formula

There is no single mathematical formula.

The general rule is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Common Figure Property}\rightarrow\text{Exception}}
$$

### Common Figure Properties

| Property | What to Check |
|---|---|
| Shape | Circle, triangle, square, etc. |
| Sides | Number of sides |
| Lines | Number of straight/curved lines |
| Dots | Number and position of dots |
| Shading | Filled/unfilled sections |
| Direction | Arrow orientation |
| Position | Top, bottom, left, right |
| Rotation | Orientation of the figure |
| Symmetry | Vertical/horizontal symmetry |
| Size | Large/small |
| Internal elements | Shapes inside shapes |
| Count | Number of components |

---

# 4. Important Properties

1. Compare all figures systematically.
2. Start with simple structural properties.
3. Count sides, lines, dots, and components.
4. Check orientation and rotation.
5. Check shading.
6. Check symmetry.
7. Check the position of internal elements.
8. Do not rely only on overall appearance.
9. A small dot or line can determine the classification.
10. The strongest rule should isolate one figure.

### Recommended Observation Order

$$
\boxed{
Shape
\rightarrow
Count
\rightarrow
Position
\rightarrow
Direction
\rightarrow
Shading
\rightarrow
Symmetry
\rightarrow
Rotation
}
$$

# 5. Basic Examples

## Example 1 — Number of Sides

### Question

Which figure is different?

- Triangle
- Square
- Pentagon
- Circle

### Pattern Recognition

Triangle → Polygon

Square → Polygon

Pentagon → Polygon

Circle → Not a polygon

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Circle}}
$$

---

## Example 2 — Number of Sides

### Question

Which figure is different?

- Triangle
- Square
- Pentagon
- Hexagon

Suppose the intended rule is:

> All except one have an even number of sides.

Triangle:

$$
3
$$

Square:

$$
4
$$

Pentagon:

$$
5
$$

Hexagon:

$$
6
$$

This does not isolate one figure.

> [!warning]
> Never force a classification rule that does not uniquely identify one option.

The correct approach is to inspect other properties.

---

## Example 3 — Shading

### Question

Three figures are shaded and one is unshaded.

### Pattern Recognition

If:

$$
3\ figures\rightarrow Shaded
$$

and:

$$
1\ figure\rightarrow Unshaded
$$

the unshaded figure is the exception.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{Unshaded figure}}
$$

---

## Example 4 — Number of Dots

### Question

Figures contain:

- 2 dots
- 2 dots
- 2 dots
- 3 dots

### Pattern Recognition

Three figures have:

$$
2\ dots
$$

One has:

$$
3\ dots
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Figure with 3 dots}}
$$

# 7. Advanced Examples

## Example 8 — Rotation

### Question

Four identical arrows are shown.

Three are rotations of the same orientation pattern.

One is a mirror reflection instead.

### Pattern Recognition

Rotation preserves the relative structure.

Reflection changes the handedness/orientation relationship.

Therefore, the reflected figure is the exception.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{Reflected figure}}
$$

---

## Example 9 — Symmetry

### Question

One has no vertical symmetry.

### Pattern Recognition

Common property:

\text{Vertical Symmetry}
$$

Exception:

$$
\boxed{\text{Figure without vertical symmetry}}
$$

## Example 10 — Multiple Elements

### Question

Each figure contains a triangle and a dot.

- Figure A: dot inside triangle
- Figure B: dot inside triangle
- Figure C: dot inside triangle
- Figure D: dot outside triangle

### Pattern Recognition

Three figures satisfy:

$$
Dot\in Triangle
$$

One does not.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{Figure with dot outside triangle}}
$$

# 8. Complex Figure Classification

## Example 11 — Shape + Shading

### Question

Four figures contain a square with a triangle inside.

Three figures have:

- shaded triangle
- triangle at top

One figure has:

- unshaded triangle
- triangle at bottom

### Pattern Recognition

Two properties change:

1. Shading
2. Position

Therefore the figure violating the common combination is the exception.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{Figure with unshaded triangle at bottom}}
$$

---

## Example 12 — Count + Direction

### Question

Four figures contain arrows.

Three figures contain:

- 2 arrows
- both pointing in the same direction

One figure contains:

- 2 arrows
- pointing in opposite directions

### Pattern Recognition

Common property:

$$
\text{Both arrows have same direction}
$$

Exception:

$$
\text{Arrows point in opposite directions}
$$

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{Opposite-direction figure}}
$$

# 9. Recognition Tricks

## Pattern 1 — Count First

> [!important]
> If the figures contain dots, lines, arrows, or shapes, count them first.

Check:

$$
N_1,N_2,N_3,N_4
$$

If three counts are equal and one differs, the exception is immediate.

---

## Pattern 2 — Check Orientation

> [!important]
> If the shapes are identical but rotated differently, compare orientation.

Common rotations:

$$
90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ
$$

---

## Pattern 3 — Check Reflection

> [!important]
> If one figure looks like a mirror image while the others are rotations, suspect **reflection**.

---

## Pattern 4 — Check Internal Elements

> [!important]
> Track:

- dots
- small shapes
- lines
- arrows
- shading

A small internal change may be the intended difference.

---

## Pattern 5 — Check Symmetry

> [!important]
> Ask:

> "Can I draw a vertical or horizontal line through the center and get matching halves?"

If three figures are symmetric and one is not, the asymmetric figure is likely the answer.

---

## Pattern 6 — Check Shading

> [!important]
> If shape and position are identical, compare:

$$
Shaded\leftrightarrow Unshaded
$$

---

## Pattern 7 — Check Position

> [!important]
> Track where the internal object is located:

$$
Top,\ Bottom,\ Left,\ Right,\ Center
$$

---

## Pattern 8 — Check Shape Count

> [!important]
> Count the number of geometric components.

Example:

$$
2,\ 2,\ 2,\ 3
$$

immediately suggests the figure containing 3 components.

---

# 10. Shortcuts

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 1 — Use the "5-second scan"**

Quickly inspect:

1. Shape
2. Count
3. Direction
4. Position
5. Shading

This often eliminates the answer without detailed analysis.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 2 — Track one unique feature**

Instead of comparing the whole figure, choose one feature:

> "Where is the dot?"

or:

> "Which way does the arrow point?"

This reduces visual complexity.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 3 — Count before interpreting**

If figures look complicated, count:

$$
\text{Lines + Dots + Shapes}
$$

before trying to understand the entire drawing.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 4 — Rotation test**

Mentally rotate the suspicious figure.

If it becomes identical to the others, it may belong to the same group.

If it still differs, it may be the odd one.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 5 — Reflection test**

If rotation does not work, test whether one figure is a mirror image.

This is especially useful for arrows and asymmetric shapes.

# 12. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Avoid These

### Mistake 1 — Choosing based on appearance

A figure may look different but still be a rotation of the others.

Always test rotation first.

---

### Mistake 2 — Ignoring small elements

A tiny dot or line may be the entire clue.

Always inspect internal components.

---

### Mistake 3 — Confusing rotation with reflection

Rotation:

> The figure turns.

Reflection:

> The figure flips.

These are not the same.

---

### Mistake 4 — Counting incorrectly

When a figure contains overlapping shapes, carefully count actual components rather than visually estimating.

---

### Mistake 5 — Ignoring position

A dot inside a circle may be at:

- top
- bottom
- left
- right
- center

Position can determine the answer.

---

### Mistake 6 — Using a weak rule

If one rule groups:

$$
3\ figures
$$

and another rule groups:

$$
2\ figures
$$

the first is usually stronger.

---

### Mistake 7 — Assuming only one property changes

Some questions combine:

$$
Rotation + Shading
$$

or:

$$
Position + Count
$$

Check every important feature.

# 13. Formula Sheet

## Classification Rule

$$
\boxed{\text{Common Property}\rightarrow\text{Exception}}
$$

## Rotation

$$
\boxed{90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ}
$$

## Reflection

$$
\boxed{Left\leftrightarrow Right}
$$

or:

$$
\boxed{Top\leftrightarrow Bottom}
$$

## Component Count

$$
\boxed{N=\text{number of visible components}}
$$

## General Figure Transformation

$$
\boxed{T(A)=B\Rightarrow T(C)=D}
$$

## Observation Order

$$
\boxed{
Shape\rightarrow Count\rightarrow Position\rightarrow Direction\rightarrow Shading\rightarrow Symmetry
}
$$

# 14. Quick Revision

> [!summary] One-Minute Revision

### Figure Classification

Find the figure that does not share the common visual property.

### Check in This Order

1. Shape
2. Number of elements
3. Number of sides
4. Number of lines
5. Number of dots
6. Position
7. Direction
8. Rotation
9. Reflection
10. Shading
11. Symmetry
12. Internal arrangement

### Fast Questions

Ask:

> What do three figures have in common?

> Which figure breaks that rule?

> Is the difference caused by rotation?

> Is it a reflection?

> Did an element move?

> Was something added or removed?

> Did shading change?

### Golden Memory Trick

**"Count, rotate, reflect, then inspect the details."**

# One-Line Recognition

**When several figures are given, find the strongest visual property shared by most figures and select the figure that violates that property.**

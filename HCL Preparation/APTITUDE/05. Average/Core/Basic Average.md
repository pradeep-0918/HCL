# Basic Average

## 1. Core Concept

> [!summary]
> **Average** represents the equal share of the total among all the observations.

The basic formula is:

$$
\boxed{
Average=\frac{Sum\ of\ observations}{Number\ of\ observations}
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Average=\frac{Total}{Number}
}
$$

---

# 2. Simple Example

Find the average of:

$$
10,\ 20,\ 30
$$

### Step 1 — Find the sum

$$
10+20+30=60
$$

### Step 2 — Count the numbers

There are `3` numbers.

### Step 3 — Divide

$$
Average=\frac{60}{3}
$$

$$
\boxed{20}
$$

---

# 3. Main Formula

If there are `n` observations:

$$
x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots,x_n
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{
Average=
\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}
}
$$

---

# 4. Golden Formula

> [!important]
> Memorize these three forms.

### Average

$$
\boxed{
A=\frac{S}{n}
}
$$

### Sum

$$
\boxed{
S=A\times n
}
$$

### Number of observations

$$
\boxed{
n=\frac{S}{A}
}
$$

Where:

- `A` = Average
- `S` = Sum
- `n` = Number of observations

---

# 5. Example — Find Average

Find the average of:

$$
15,\ 25,\ 35,\ 45,\ 55
$$

Sum:

$$
15+25+35+45+55=175
$$

Number:

$$
5
$$

Average:

$$
\frac{175}{5}
=
\boxed{35}
$$

---

# 6. Average as Equal Distribution

Suppose the numbers are:

$$
10,\ 20,\ 30
$$

Total:

$$
60
$$

If the total is equally distributed among `3` numbers:

$$
60/3=20
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{Average=20}
$$

> **Average means equal sharing of the total.**

---

# 7. Average Does Not Have to Be an Existing Number

Consider:

$$
10,\ 20
$$

Average:

$$
\frac{10+20}{2}
=
15
$$

`15` is not present in the list, but it is still the average.

Therefore:

> [!note]
> The average does **not** need to be one of the observations.

# 12. Finding Missing Number

The average of:

$$
10,\ 20,\ 30,\ x
$$

is `25`.

There are `4` numbers.

Total required:

$$

$$

$$
\boxed{x=40}

# 25. Average and Deviation

A useful way to solve aptitude problems is to choose a convenient reference value.

Suppose:

$$
48,\ 49,\ 50,\ 51,\ 52
$$

Take `50` as reference.

Deviations:

$$
-2,-1,0,+1,+2
$$

Their sum:

$$
0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{Average=50}
$$

---

# 26. Deviation Method

If we choose an assumed average `A₀`, then:

$$
\boxed{
Actual\ Average
=
A_0+\frac{Sum\ of\ deviations}{n}
}
$$

This is especially useful when numbers are close together.

---

# 27. Example — Deviation Method

Find average:

$$
98,\ 99,\ 100,\ 101,\ 102
$$

Take assumed average:

$$
100
$$

Deviations:

$$
-2,-1,0,+1,+2
$$

Sum:

$$
0
$$

Therefore:

$$
100+\frac05
=
\boxed{100}
$$

---

# 30. Average of First n Natural Numbers

First `n` natural numbers:

$$
1,2,3,\ldots,n
$$

Their sum:

$$
\frac{n(n+1)}2
$$

Therefore average:

$$
\frac{\frac{n(n+1)}2}{n}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\frac{n+1}{2}
}
$$

Example for `1 to 10`:

$$
\frac{10+1}{2}
=
\boxed{5.5}
$$

# 39. Average and Maximum/Minimum

The average of a set lies between its minimum and maximum values.

If:

$$
10\le x_i\le50
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{10\le Average\le50}
$$

> [!warning]
> An average cannot normally be greater than the maximum or smaller than the minimum.

---

# 40. Average and Outliers

Suppose:

$$
10,\ 10,\ 10,\ 100
$$

Average:

$$
130/4
=
32.5
$$

Although most values are `10`, the large value `100` increases the average.

> [!important]
> Average is sensitive to extreme values.

---

# 41. Removing a Number

If the average of `n` numbers is `A`, total is:

$$
nA
$$

If a number `x` is removed:

New total:

$$
nA-x
$$

New number of values:

$$
n-1
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
New\ Average=
\frac{nA-x}{n-1}
}
$$

---

# 42. Adding a Number

If average of `n` numbers is `A`, and `x` is added:

Old total:

$$
nA
$$

New total:

$$
nA+x
$$

New count:

$$
n+1
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
New\ Average=
\frac{nA+x}{n+1}
}
$$

---

# 43. Quick Intuition

If a number added is:

- **greater than current average** → average increases
- **less than current average** → average decreases
- **equal to current average** → average stays unchanged

This is extremely important.

---

# 44. Example — Addition

Average of 5 numbers = `20`.

A new number `30` is added.

Old total:

$$
5\times20=100
$$

New total:

$$
100+30=130
$$

New count:

$$
6
$$

New average:

$$
130/6
=
\boxed{21.67}
$$

Average increased.

---

# 45. Example — Removal

Average of 6 numbers = `25`.

One number `40` is removed.

Old total:

$$
6\times25=150
$$

New total:

$$
150-40=110
$$

New count:

$$
5
$$

New average:

$$
110/5
=
\boxed{22}
$$

Average decreased.

---

# 46. Equal Addition

If the same number `k` is added to every observation:

$$
x_1+k,\ x_2+k,\ldots,x_n+k
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{New\ Average=Old\ Average+k}
$$

Example:

Average = `20`.

Add `5` to every number.

New average:

$$
\boxed{25}
$$

---

# 47. Equal Subtraction

If `k` is subtracted from every observation:

$$
\boxed{New\ Average=Old\ Average-k}
$$

Example:

Average = `50`.

Subtract `8` from every value.

New average:

$$
\boxed{42}
$$

---

# 48. Multiplying Every Number

If every observation is multiplied by `k`:

$$
x_1k,x_2k,\ldots,x_nk
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{New\ Average=k\times Old\ Average}
$$

Example:

Average = `15`.

Every value is doubled.

New average:

$$
\boxed{30}
$$

---

# 49. Dividing Every Number

If every observation is divided by `k`:

$$
\boxed{
New\ Average=\frac{Old\ Average}{k}
}
$$

Example:

Average = `80`.

Every value is divided by `4`.

New average:

$$
\boxed{20}
$$

---

# 50. Linear Transformation

If every number changes from:

$$
x
$$

to:

$$
ax+b
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{
New\ Average=a(Old\ Average)+b
}
$$

Example:

Old average = `20`.

Every number becomes:

$$
3x+5
$$

New average:

$$
3(20)+5
=
\boxed{65}
$$

---

# 51. Important Shortcut

> [!tip]
> **Any operation applied equally to every observation can usually be applied directly to the average.**

Examples:

$$
x\rightarrow x+5
$$

Average:

$$
A\rightarrow A+5
$$

$$
x\rightarrow2x
$$

Average:

$$
A\rightarrow2A
$$

---

# 52. Average of Two Groups

Group 1:

- Number of observations = `n₁`
- Average = `A₁`

Group 2:

- Number of observations = `n₂`
- Average = `A₂`

Combined average:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{n_1A_1+n_2A_2}{n_1+n_2}
}
$$

This is the basic combined-average formula.

---

# 53. Example — Two Groups

Class A:

`20` students, average `60`.

Class B:

`30` students, average `70`.

Combined average:

$$
\frac{20(60)+30(70)}{20+30}
$$

$$
=\frac{1200+2100}{50}
$$

$$
=\frac{3300}{50}
$$

$$
\boxed{66}
$$

---

# 54. Why Simple Average of Averages Can Be Wrong

Class A:

20 students, average `60`.

Class B:

30 students, average `70`.

Wrong:

$$
\frac{60+70}{2}=65
$$

Correct:

$$
\boxed{66}
$$

Why?

Because the groups have different sizes.

---

# 55. When Can We Average the Averages?

If the groups have **equal numbers of observations**, then:

$$
\boxed{
Combined\ Average=
\frac{A_1+A_2}{2}
}
$$

Example:

Group A: 20 students, average 60.

Group B: 20 students, average 70.

Combined:

$$
\frac{60+70}{2}
=
\boxed{65}
$$

---

# 56. Weighted Average

> [!summary]
> A weighted average gives different importance to different values.

Formula:

$$
\boxed{
Weighted\ Average=
\frac{\sum wx}{\sum w}
}
$$

where:

- `x` = value
- `w` = weight

---

# 57. Simple Weighted Example

Marks:

- Exam = 80, weight 60%
- Assignment = 70, weight 40%

Weighted average:

$$
80(0.6)+70(0.4)
$$

$$
48+28
$$

$$
\boxed{76}
$$

# 74. Average of Rates — Warning

You cannot always simply average rates.

Example:

A person travels equal distances at:

$$
40\text{ km/h}
$$

and:

$$
60\text{ km/h}
$$

The average speed is **not**:

$$
\frac{40+60}{2}=50
$$

For equal distances, the correct average speed is:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{2ab}{a+b}
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{2(40)(60)}{40+60}
=
\boxed{48\text{ km/h}}
$$

> [!important]
> Average of rates depends on the weights/conditions.

---

# 75. Average Speed — Basic Principle

For any journey:

$$
\boxed{
Average\ Speed=
\frac{Total\ Distance}{Total\ Time}
}
$$

Do not blindly average individual speeds.

\boxed{45}
$$

No need to calculate the total.

# 86. Important Shortcut — Assumed Average

When numbers are close:

1. Choose a convenient value.
2. Find deviations.
3. Add deviations.
4. Divide by number of observations.
5. Adjust the assumed average.

Formula:

$$
\boxed{
Actual\ Average=
Assumed\ Average+
\frac{\sum d}{n}
}
$$

# 87. Average and Frequency

If a value occurs multiple times, treat its occurrence as its weight.

Example:

`10` occurs 2 times.

`20` occurs 3 times.

`30` occurs 5 times.

Average:

$$
\frac{10(2)+20(3)+30(5)}{2+3+5}
$$

$$
=\frac{20+60+150}{10}
$$

$$
\boxed{23}
$$

# 88. Frequency Formula

If values are:

$$
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n
$$

with frequencies:

$$
f_1,f_2,\ldots,f_n
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{
Average=
\frac{\sum fx}{\sum f}
}
$$

This is a weighted average.

# 90. Average and Range

Range:

$$
Maximum-Minimum
$$

Average is not the range.

Range:

# 91. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Avoid These

### Mistake 1 — Forgetting to divide by count

Wrong:

$$
10+20+30=60
$$

Average is not `60`.

Correct:

$$
60/3=20
$$

---

### Mistake 2 — Averaging averages blindly

Always consider group sizes.

---

### Mistake 3 — Using average of speeds

Use:

$$
\frac{Total\ Distance}{Total\ Time}
$$

when dealing with average speed.

---

### Mistake 4 — Forgetting the changed count

When one value is added:

$$
n\rightarrow n+1
$$

When one is removed:

$$
n\rightarrow n-1
$$

---

### Mistake 5 — Ignoring replacement

For replacement:

$$
\boxed{
New\ Total=Old\ Total-Old\ Value+New\ Value
}
$$

# 95. Aptitude Speed Tricks

> [!tip] Exam Strategy

### Trick 1
For consecutive numbers:

> **Average = First + Last ÷ 2**

### Trick 2
If every value increases by `k`:

> **Average increases by `k`.**

### Trick 3
If every value is multiplied by `k`:

> **Average is multiplied by `k`.**

### Trick 4
If one value is replaced:

> **Average change = Change in value ÷ Number of observations.**

### Trick 5
For groups with different sizes:

> **Never simply average the group averages.**

### Trick 6
For average speed:

> **Total distance ÷ Total time.**

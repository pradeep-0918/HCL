# Mixed Percentage Problems

## 1. Core Concept

> [!summary]
> Mixed percentage problems combine multiple percentage concepts in a single question.

They may involve:

- successive increase/decrease
- price and consumption
- salary and expenditure
- marks
- population
- profit/loss
- discount
- reverse percentage
- percentage comparison
- percentage error
- percentage difference
- changing bases
- multiple quantities changing simultaneously

The most powerful method is:

> **Convert every percentage change into a multiplier.**

---

# 2. The Multiplier Method

For an increase of `r%`:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Multiplier}=1+\frac r{100}
}
$$

For a decrease of `r%`:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Multiplier}=1-\frac r{100}
}
$$

Then multiply the factors.

---

# 3. Basic Example

A value increases by `20%` and then by `30%`.

Multipliers:

$$
1.2,\quad1.3
$$

Combined:

$$
1.2\times1.3
=
1.56
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{56\%\text{ increase}}
$$

---

# 4. Successive Increase Formula

For increases of `a%` and `b%`:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Net Increase}
=
a+b+\frac{ab}{100}
}
$$

Example:

`20%` and `30%`:

$$
20+30+\frac{20\times30}{100}
$$

$$
=50+6
$$

$$
\boxed{56\%}
$$

---

# 5. Successive Decrease Formula

For decreases of `a%` and `b%`:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Net Decrease}
=
a+b-\frac{ab}{100}
}
$$

Example:

`20%` and `30%`:

$$
20+30-6
=
\boxed{44\%}
$$

---

# 6. Increase Followed by Decrease

Increase by `20%`, then decrease by `10%`.

Multiplier:

$$
1.2\times0.9
=
1.08
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{8\%\text{ increase}}
$$

---

# 7. Equal Increase and Decrease

Increase by `x%`, then decrease by `x%`.

Net:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Loss}=
\frac{x^2}{100}\%
}
$$

Example:

`20%` increase and `20%` decrease:

$$
\frac{20^2}{100}
=
\boxed{4\%\text{ decrease}}
$$

---

# 8. Equal Decrease and Increase

Decrease by `20%`, then increase by `20%`.

$$
0.8\times1.2
=
0.96
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{4\%\text{ decrease}}
$$

Order does not matter for multiplication when the same two percentage changes are applied.

---

# 9. Finding the Required Reverse Increase

A value decreases by `20%`.

What percentage increase is required to return to the original value?

After decrease:

$$
100\rightarrow80
$$

Required increase:

$$
\frac{20}{80}\times100
=
\boxed{25\%}
$$

---

# 10. General Reverse Formula

After a decrease of `d%`, required increase:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{d}{100-d}\times100\%
}
$$

Example:

Decrease = `25%`

$$
\frac{25}{75}\times100
=
\boxed{33\frac13\%}
$$

---

# 11. Reverse After Increase

A value increases by `25%`.

What percentage decrease returns it to the original value?

Original:

$$
100
$$

New:

$$
125
$$

Decrease required:

$$
\frac{25}{125}\times100
=
\boxed{20\%}
$$

General formula:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Required decrease}
=
\frac{i}{100+i}\times100\%
}
$$

# 14. Salary + Expenditure

Salary increases by `20%`.

Expenditure increases by `10%`.

Suppose:

Initial salary:

$$
₹50,000
$$

Initial expenditure:

$$
₹30,000
$$

New salary:

$$
50000\times1.2
=
₹60,000
$$

New expenditure:

$$
30000\times1.1
=
₹33,000
$$

Old savings:

$$
50000-30000
=
₹20,000
$$

New savings:

$$
60000-33000
=
₹27,000
$$

Savings increase:

$$
7000
$$

Percentage increase:

$$
\frac{7000}{20000}\times100
=
\boxed{35\%}
$$

# 15. Salary + Expenditure Shortcut

> [!tip]
> When two quantities change, **do not calculate percentage change of the difference directly**.

Instead:

1. Find old values.
2. Apply multipliers.
3. Find new difference.
4. Compare old and new difference.

# 29. Mixed Changes Shortcut

For multiple changes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Final multiplier}
=
\prod
\left(1\pm\frac r{100}\right)
}
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Net percentage change}
=
(\text{Final multiplier}-1)\times100
}
$$

$$

=

$$

$$

=
100
$$

$$

$$

$$

=

\boxed{

\frac{20\times30}{100}

# 32. Percentage Change in a Percentage

Suppose pass percentage changes from:

$$
40\%\rightarrow50\%
$$

Absolute change:

$$
10\text{ percentage points}
$$

Relative percentage increase:

$$
\frac{10}{40}\times100
=

$$

# 33. Percentage of a Percentage — Common Trap

If A is `20%` of B and B is `30%` of C:

$$
A=20\%\times30\%\times C
$$

$$
=0.2\times0.3C
$$

$$
=0.06C
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{A=6\%\text{ of C}}
$$

---

# 34. Base Change

Suppose:

A is `25%` more than B.

Then:

$$
A=1.25B
$$

Therefore B relative to A:

$$
B=\frac{A}{1.25}
=
0.8A
$$

So B is:

$$
\boxed{20\%\text{ less than A}}
$$

---

# 35. More/Less Conversion

If A is `x%` more than B:

$$
A=(1+x/100)B
$$

Then B is less than A by:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{x}{100+x}\times100\%
}
$$

---

# 36. Less/More Conversion

If A is `x%` less than B:

$$
A=(1-x/100)B
$$

Then B is more than A by:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{x}{100-x}\times100\%
}
$$

---

# 37. Example

A is `40%` more than B.

Then B is less than A by:

$$
\frac{40}{140}\times100
=
\boxed{28\frac47\%}
$$

---

# 38. Example

A is `25%` less than B.

Then B is more than A by:

$$

=
\boxed{33\frac13\%}
$$

# 39. Error + Percentage

Actual value:

$$
500
$$

Measured value:

$$
490
$$

Percentage error:

$$
\frac{10}{500}\times100
=
\boxed{2\%}

# 47. Finding an Unknown Percentage

A value increases by `20%`, then by `x%`, resulting in a total increase of `50%`.

Equation:

$$
1.2\left(1+\frac{x}{100}\right)
=
1.5
$$

Therefore:

$$
1+\frac{x}{100}
=
\frac{1.5}{1.2}
=
1.25
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{x=25\%}
$$

---

# 48. Finding Unknown Decrease

A value increases by `20%` and then decreases by `x%`, ending at the original value.

$$
1.2(1-x/100)=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
1-x/100
=
\frac56
$$

So:

$$
x=
\boxed{16\frac23\%}
$$

---

# 49. Finding Unknown Increase

A value decreases by `20%` and then increases by `x%`, returning to the original.

$$
0.8(1+x/100)=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
1+x/100=1.25
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{x=25\%}
$$

---

# 50. Equal Final Value

If:

- A increases by `20%`
- B decreases by `20%`
- final values become equal

Then:

$$
1.2A=0.8B
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac AB=\frac{0.8}{1.2}
=
\frac23
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{A:B=2:3}
$$

---

# 51. Mixed Ratio + Percentage

A is `20%` more than B.

Therefore:

$$
A:B=120:100
=
\boxed{6:5}
$$

$$
A=600
$$

# 52. Percentage and Ratio Conversion

If:

$$
A:B=m:n
$$

Then percentage by which A is more than B:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{m-n}{n}\times100
}
$$

Percentage by which B is less than A:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{m-n}{m}\times100
}
$$

---

# 53. Example — Ratio 5:4

A:B = `5:4`.

A is more than B by:

$$
\frac{5-4}{4}\times100
=
\boxed{25\%}
$$

B is less than A by:

$$
\frac{5-4}{5}\times100
=
\boxed{20\%}
$$

Percentage difference:

$$
\frac{2(1)}9\times100
=
\boxed{22.22\%}
$$

# 56. Common Exam Traps

> [!warning] Very Important

### Trap 1 — Adding successive changes

Wrong:

$$
20\%+30\%=50\%
$$

Correct:

$$
1.2\times1.3
=
1.56
$$

$$
\boxed{56\%}
$$

---

### Trap 2 — Equal increase/decrease

`20% increase + 20% decrease`

does NOT give `0%`.

Correct:

$$
\boxed{4\%\text{ decrease}}
$$

---

### Trap 3 — Same percentage in reverse

A `20%` decrease requires a `25%` increase to recover.

---

### Trap 4 — Wrong base

A is `20% more than B` does not mean B is `20% less than A`.

---

### Trap 5 — Average percentage

Do not average percentages when weights are different.

---

### Trap 6 — Constant expenditure

Do not use the same opposite percentage.

`20% price increase → 16⅔% consumption decrease`.

---

### Trap 7 — Percentage difference

Do not use one value as denominator.

Use the average.

---

### Trap 8 — Percentage error

Use the true value as denominator.

$$
\boxed{
Q\uparrow25\%
}
$$

### A is `20%` more than B

B is:

$$
\boxed{16\frac23\%\text{ less than A}}
$$

### Percentage Difference

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\text{Difference}}{\text{Average}}\times100
}
$$

### Percentage Error

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\text{Absolute Error}}{\text{True Value}}\times100
}
$$

# 62. Final Recognition Rule

> [!success]
> **When a percentage problem contains multiple changes, STOP calculating percentages directly. Convert every change into a multiplier first.**

### Mental process:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Percentage}
\rightarrow
\text{Multiplier}
\rightarrow
\text{Multiply/Divide}
\rightarrow
\text{Final Multiplier}
\rightarrow
\text{Answer}
}
$$

This is the **master pattern** for almost every mixed-percentage aptitude question.

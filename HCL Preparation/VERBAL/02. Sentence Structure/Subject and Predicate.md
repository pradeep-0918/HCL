# Subject and Predicate

## 1. Core Concept

> [!summary]
> Every complete sentence normally has two main parts: **Subject** and **Predicate**.
>
> **Subject = who or what the sentence is about**
>
> **Predicate = what the subject does, is, has, or what is said about the subject**

### Basic Structure

$$
\boxed{\text{Sentence} = \text{Subject} + \text{Predicate}}
$$

Example:

> The student solved the problem.

Subject:

> The student

Predicate:

> solved the problem.

### Core Intuition

Ask two questions:

1. **Who/What?** → Subject
2. **What about it? / What does it do?** → Predicate

---

# 2. Basic Meaning

## Subject

The **subject** is the person, thing, place, idea, or group that performs the action or is being described.

Examples:

> **Ravi** plays cricket.

> **The dog** is barking.

> **My computer** is slow.

> **Honesty** is important.

The bold part is the subject.

---

## Predicate

The **predicate** contains the verb and gives information about the subject.

Examples:

> Ravi **plays cricket**.

> The dog **is barking**.

> My computer **is slow**.

> Honesty **is important**.

The bold part is the predicate.

---

# 3. Main Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{Complete Sentence} = \text{Subject} + \text{Predicate}}
$$

### Example

> The boy / is reading a book.

| Part | Text |
|---|---|
| Subject | The boy |
| Predicate | is reading a book |

---

# 4. Important Properties

1. The subject tells us who or what the sentence is about.
2. The predicate tells us something about the subject.
3. The predicate always contains a verb in a complete sentence.
4. The subject may contain more than one word.
5. The predicate may contain many words.
6. The subject can appear after the verb in some sentence structures.
7. The subject can be understood rather than directly written in imperative sentences.
8. A sentence can have a compound subject.
9. A sentence can have a compound predicate.
10. The subject determines subject-verb agreement.
11. The main noun/pronoun performing the grammatical role is called the **simple subject**.
12. The complete subject can contain modifiers.
13. The complete predicate can contain objects, complements, and modifiers.

---

# 5. Complete Subject

The **complete subject** includes the main subject plus all words that describe or modify it.

Example:

> The tall boy in the blue shirt won the race.

Complete Subject:

> The tall boy in the blue shirt

Complete Predicate:

> won the race.

### Structure

$$
\boxed{\text{Complete Subject} + \text{Complete Predicate}}
$$

---

# 6. Simple Subject

The **simple subject** is the main word, usually a noun or pronoun, that the sentence is about.

Example:

> The tall boy in the blue shirt won the race.

Simple Subject:

> boy

Complete Subject:

> The tall boy in the blue shirt

Predicate:

> won the race.

### Important

> [!important]
> **Simple Subject = Core noun/pronoun**
>
> **Complete Subject = Simple Subject + modifiers**

---

# 7. Complete Predicate

The complete predicate contains the verb and all words connected to what is being said about the subject.

Example:

> The students completed the difficult assignment before lunch.

Complete Subject:

> The students

Complete Predicate:

> completed the difficult assignment before lunch.

---

# 8. Simple Predicate

The **simple predicate** is the main verb or verb phrase of the sentence.

Example:

> The students have completed the difficult assignment.

Simple Predicate:

> have completed

Complete Predicate:

> have completed the difficult assignment

### Important

$$
\boxed{\text{Simple Predicate} = \text{Main Verb/Verb Phrase}}
$$

---

# 9. Subject vs Predicate

| Feature | Subject | Predicate |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | Who/What? | What does the subject do/is? |
| Main role | Topic/performer | Action/information |
| Usually contains | Noun/Pronoun | Verb |
| Example | The boy | plays cricket |
| Position | Often before verb | Usually after subject |

# 12. Compound Subject

A sentence can have more than one subject.

Example:

> Ravi and Arun play cricket.

Subjects:

> Ravi

> Arun

Together:

> Ravi and Arun

This is called a **compound subject**.

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{Subject}_1 + \text{and} + \text{Subject}_2 + \text{Predicate}}
$$

Example:

> Riya and Priya are studying.

Compound Subject:

> Riya and Priya

Predicate:

> are studying

---

# 13. Compound Subject with Different Nouns

Examples:

> The teacher and the students entered the classroom.

> John and Mary are working together.

> Mathematics and physics are difficult subjects.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If two or more nouns/pronouns perform the same action, you may have a **compound subject**.

---

# 14. Compound Predicate

A sentence can have one subject performing multiple actions.

Example:

> Ravi opened the book and started reading.

Subject:

> Ravi

Compound Predicate:

> opened the book and started reading

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{One Subject} + \text{Multiple Predicates}}
$$

---

# 15. Compound Subject vs Compound Predicate

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Compound Subject | Multiple subjects | Ravi and Arun play. |
| Compound Predicate | Multiple actions | Ravi opened and read. |
| Both | Multiple subjects + actions | Ravi and Arun opened and read the books. |

### Quick Trick

> [!tip]
> Ask:
>
> **Who is doing it?**
>
> If multiple → Compound Subject.
>
> **How many actions?**
>
> If multiple → Compound Predicate.

---

# 16. Subject and Predicate with Auxiliary Verbs

The predicate may contain multiple verbs.

Example:

> She has been studying English.

Subject:

> She

Complete Predicate:

> has been studying English

Simple Predicate:

> has been studying

### Verb Phrase

$$
\boxed{\text{Auxiliary Verbs} + \text{Main Verb}}
$$

---

# 17. Subject and Predicate with Objects

Example:

> The student solved the problem.

Subject:

> The student

Predicate:

> solved the problem

Inside the predicate:

> solved = verb

> the problem = object

This leads to an important distinction:

$$
\boxed{\text{Subject} \neq \text{Object}}
$$

---

# 18. Subject vs Object

### Subject

Performs the action.

> Ravi kicked the ball.

Subject:

> Ravi

### Object

Receives the action.

> Ravi kicked the ball.

Object:

> the ball

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{Subject} + \text{Verb} + \text{Object}}
$$

---

# 19. Subject-Verb Agreement Connection

The subject determines the form of the verb in many sentences.

Example:

> The boy **plays** cricket.

> The boys **play** cricket.

The difference is caused by the subject.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> In error-detection questions, identify the **true subject** before checking the verb.

---

# 20. Tricky Subject — Ignore Prepositional Phrases

This is a very important aptitude pattern.

Example:

> The box of chocolates is on the table.

Many students incorrectly choose:

> chocolates

as the subject.

Correct subject:

> box

Why?

`of chocolates` is a prepositional phrase modifying `box`.

Correct:

> The box of chocolates **is** on the table.

Not:

> The box of chocolates **are** on the table.

---

# 21. Subject Recognition with "Of"

### Pattern

> The + noun + of + noun + verb

The first noun is often the main subject.

Example:

> The list of items is ready.

Subject:

> list

Not:

> items

Example:

> A group of students is waiting.

Subject:

> group

Not:

> students

> [!tip]
> When checking subject-verb agreement, do not let the noun after **of** distract you from the actual subject.

---

# 22. Subject with Prepositional Phrases

Example:

> The students in the classroom are studying.

Complete Subject:

> The students in the classroom

Simple Subject:

> students

Predicate:

> are studying

`in the classroom` is a prepositional phrase.

---

# 23. Subject After the Verb

The subject is not always the first element.

Example:

> There are many problems.

The real subject is:

> many problems

The word `there` is not the main subject.

### Another Example

> There is a book on the table.

Subject:

> a book

Predicate:

> is on the table

---

# 24. "There" Construction

This is frequently useful in error detection.

### Singular

> There is a problem.

Subject:

> a problem

### Plural

> There are many problems.

Subject:

> many problems

### Recognition

> [!important]
> In `there is/are` constructions, look after `is/are` to identify the actual subject.

---

# 25. Questions — Finding the Subject

Question sentences can make subject identification harder.

Example:

> Who broke the window?

Subject:

> Who

Predicate:

> broke the window

Another example:

> What caused the accident?

Subject:

> What

Predicate:

> caused the accident

---

# 26. Subject in Questions

Example:

> Did Ravi complete the assignment?

Subject:

> Ravi

Predicate:

> did complete the assignment

The auxiliary `did` comes before the subject because it is a question.

### Normal Statement

> Ravi completed the assignment.

### Question

> Did Ravi complete the assignment?

> [!important]
> Do not assume the first word is always the subject.

---

# 27. Imperative Sentences

Commands often have an understood subject.

Example:

> Open the door.

The subject is understood as:

> you

Complete meaning:

> You open the door.

Another:

> Please sit down.

Understood subject:

> you

### Important

> [!important]
> In imperative sentences, the subject **you** is often omitted but understood.

---

# 28. Exclamatory Sentences

Example:

> What a beautiful day it is!

Subject:

> it

Predicate:

> is a beautiful day

Another:

> How beautiful the flowers are!

Subject:

> the flowers

Predicate:

> are beautiful

---

# 29. Negative Sentences

Example:

> The boy does not like coffee.

Subject:

> The boy

Predicate:

> does not like coffee

The negative word `not` remains inside the predicate.

---

# 30. Interrogative Sentences

Example:

> Is the manager attending the meeting?

Subject:

> the manager

Predicate:

> is attending the meeting

### Recognition

Even when the verb comes before the subject, identify the noun/pronoun that performs or experiences the action.

---

# 31. Compound Subject with Agreement

Example:

> Ravi and Arun are friends.

Two subjects:

> Ravi + Arun

Therefore:

> are

Not:

> is

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{Two subjects joined by and} \rightarrow \text{usually plural verb}}
$$

---

# 32. Compound Subject — Important Exception

Subjects joined by `and` can sometimes refer to one combined idea or one person/thing.

Example:

> Bread and butter is my breakfast.

Here `bread and butter` is treated as one combined item/meal.

Another example:

> The owner and manager is arriving.

If the same person is both owner and manager, singular treatment can be possible.

> [!warning]
> Do not apply "and = plural" mechanically. Meaning matters.

---

# 33. Subject with "Each" and "Every"

Example:

> Each student is responsible.

Subject:

> Each student

Verb:

> is

Example:

> Every student has a book.

Verb:

> has

### Recognition

> [!important]
> `Each` and `Every` normally take a **singular noun and singular verb**.

---

# 34. Subject with "One of"

Example:

> One of the students is absent.

Actual subject:

> One

Not:

> students

Therefore:

> is

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{One of + plural noun + singular verb}}
$$

Example:

> One of my friends is a doctor.

---

# 35. Subject with "A Number of" and "The Number of"

This is a major error-detection pattern.

### A number of

Meaning:

> many

Takes plural verb.

> A number of students are absent.

### The number of

Meaning:

> the total/count

Takes singular verb.

> The number of students is increasing.

### Quick Trick

$$
\boxed{\text{A number of} \rightarrow \text{Plural}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{The number of} \rightarrow \text{Singular}}
$$

---

# 36. Subject with Collective Nouns

Collective nouns refer to groups.

Examples:

- team
- family
- committee
- audience
- class
- government

Example:

> The team is winning.

When the group is treated as one unit, singular agreement is common.

In some varieties of English, especially British usage, a collective noun may take a plural verb when individual members are emphasized.

> The team are arguing among themselves.

> [!important]
> For placement grammar, first determine whether the group is treated as a single unit or as individual members.

---

# 37. Subject with "As Well As"

Example:

> The manager, as well as the employees, is attending the meeting.

Main subject:

> The manager

`as well as the employees` does not make the subject plural.

Correct:

> is attending

### Compare

> The manager and the employees are attending.

Here `and` creates a compound subject.

---

# 38. Subject with "Along With"

Example:

> Ravi, along with his friends, is going to the event.

Main subject:

> Ravi

Therefore:

> is

Not:

> are

### Similar expressions

- along with
- together with
- as well as
- accompanied by
- in addition to

These phrases generally do not change the number of the main subject.

---

# 39. Subject with "Either...Or"

Example:

> Either Ravi or Arun is responsible.

When alternatives have the same number:

> singular + singular → singular verb

Example:

> Either the teacher or the student is responsible.

---

# 40. Subject with "Neither...Nor"

Example:

> Neither Ravi nor Arun is available.

Both subjects are singular.

Therefore:

> is

### Advanced Rule

When the subjects have different numbers, the verb generally agrees with the subject closer to it.

Example:

> Neither the teacher nor the students are available.

Closer subject:

> students

Therefore:

> are

Another:

> Neither the students nor the teacher is available.

Closer subject:

> teacher

Therefore:

> is

> [!important]
> **Either...or / Neither...nor → check the nearer subject when the numbers differ.**

---

# 41. Subject with Relative Clauses

Example:

> The students who study regularly perform well.

Main subject:

> students

`who study regularly` is a relative clause describing `students`.

Predicate:

> perform well

### Important

Do not confuse a noun inside a relative clause with the main subject.

---

# 42. Subject with Long Modifiers

Example:

> The quality of the products manufactured by this company is excellent.

Main subject:

> quality

Not:

> products

Not:

> company

Predicate:

> is excellent

### Recognition

> [!tip]
> Strip away extra descriptive phrases and find the core noun.

---

# 43. Subject-Verb Agreement — Core Connection

### Singular Subject

Usually takes singular verb.

> The boy plays.

### Plural Subject

Usually takes plural verb.

> The boys play.

### Third Person Singular Present

Common pattern:

$$
\boxed{He/She/It + V1+s/es}
$$

Example:

> She works.

### Plural

$$
\boxed{They/We/You + V1}
$$

Example:

> They work.

---

# 44. Finding the Subject — Fast Method

> [!tip]
> **Subject Identification Shortcut**
>
> Step 1: Find the main verb.
>
> Step 2: Ask:
>
> **Who/What performs the action?**
>
> Step 3: Ignore distracting prepositional phrases.
>
> Step 4: Identify the noun/pronoun connected to the verb.
>
> Step 5: Check whether the subject is singular or plural.

### Example

> The box of old books is heavy.

Main verb:

> is

Ask:

> What is heavy?

Answer:

> box

Subject:

> box

# 45. Pattern Recognition

## Pattern 1 — Basic Subject

### How to Recognize

Ask:

> Who/What performs the action?

Example:

> Ravi plays cricket.

Who plays?

> Ravi

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Subject = Ravi}}
$$

---

## Pattern 2 — Basic Predicate

### How to Recognize

After finding the subject, ask:

> What is being said about the subject?

Example:

> Ravi plays cricket.

Predicate:

> plays cricket

---

## Pattern 3 — Long Subject

### How to Recognize

Look for a noun plus descriptive words.

Example:

> The intelligent student from Chennai won the prize.

Simple Subject:

> student

Complete Subject:

> The intelligent student from Chennai

---

## Pattern 4 — Long Predicate

### How to Recognize

Everything that tells us what the subject does/is generally belongs to the predicate.

Example:

> The manager approved the proposal after discussion.

Predicate:

> approved the proposal after discussion

---

## Pattern 5 — Compound Subject

### How to Recognize

Two or more nouns/pronouns perform the same action.

Example:

> Ravi and Arun play cricket.

Think:

$$
\boxed{\text{Compound Subject}}
$$

---

## Pattern 6 — Compound Predicate

### How to Recognize

One subject performs multiple actions.

Example:

> Ravi opened the book and started reading.

Think:

$$
\boxed{\text{Compound Predicate}}
$$

---

## Pattern 7 — "Of" Trap

### How to Recognize

If you see:

> noun + of + noun

Do not automatically choose the second noun as subject.

Example:

> The box of chocolates is heavy.

Subject:

> box

---

## Pattern 8 — "There Is/Are"

### How to Recognize

If a sentence starts with:

> There is...

or:

> There are...

look after the verb for the actual subject.

Example:

> There are many students.

Subject:

> many students

---

## Pattern 9 — "One of"

### How to Recognize

If you see:

> one of + plural noun

Think:

$$
\boxed{\text{Singular Subject}}
$$

Example:

> One of the students is absent.

---

## Pattern 10 — "A Number of"

### How to Recognize

If you see:

> a number of + plural noun

Think:

$$
\boxed{\text{Plural Verb}}
$$

Example:

> A number of students are absent.

---

## Pattern 11 — "The Number of"

### How to Recognize

If you see:

> the number of + plural noun

Think:

$$
\boxed{\text{Singular Verb}}
$$

Example:

> The number of students is increasing.

---

## Pattern 12 — "As Well As"

### How to Recognize

If you see:

> Subject + as well as + extra noun

The first subject controls the verb.

Example:

> Ravi, as well as his friends, is attending.

---

## Pattern 13 — Either...Or

### How to Recognize

Check the subjects connected by `or`.

If numbers differ, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject.

Example:

> Either the teacher or the students are responsible.

---

## Pattern 14 — Neither...Nor

### How to Recognize

Check the nearest subject when the subjects differ in number.

Example:

> Neither the students nor the teacher is available.

---

## Pattern 15 — Imperative Sentence

### How to Recognize

A command often has an unstated subject.

Example:

> Open the door.

Understood subject:

> you

`A number of` → plural.

> A number of employees are absent.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{is} \rightarrow \text{are}}
$$

## Example 40

### Question

> The number of applications are increasing.

`The number of` → singular.

Correct:

> The number of applications is increasing.

### Answer

\boxed{\text{are} \rightarrow \text{is}}

# 49. Subject Identification — Advanced Method

When a sentence is long, use the **Core Extraction Method**.

### Step 1

Find the main verb.

### Step 2

Remove introductory phrases.

### Step 3

Ignore prepositional phrases.

### Step 4

Ignore relative clauses temporarily.

### Step 5

Find the core noun/pronoun connected to the verb.

### Step 6

Check whether it is singular or plural.

### Example

> The collection of rare books in the university library is extremely valuable.

Remove:

> of rare books

Remove:

> in the university library

Core:

> The collection is valuable.

Subject:

> collection

Verb:

> is

# 50. Subject-Predicate Tree

A simple sentence can be represented as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sentence}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Subject}\\
\text{Predicate}
\end{cases}
}
$$

Example:

> The smart student solved the difficult problem.

\text{Sentence}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Subject: The smart student}\\
\text{Predicate: solved the difficult problem}
\end{cases}
$$

# 54. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Avoid These

### Mistake 1 — Assuming the First Word Is the Subject

Incorrect approach:

> There are many students.

Choosing `There` as the subject.

Correct:

> many students

---

### Mistake 2 — Choosing the Noun After "Of"

Incorrect:

> The box of chocolates are...

Choosing `chocolates`.

Correct:

> The box ... is.

---

### Mistake 3 — Confusing Subject and Object

> Ravi kicked the ball.

Subject:

> Ravi

Object:

> ball

---

### Mistake 4 — Ignoring Modifiers

> The tall boy in the classroom won.

Simple Subject:

> boy

Complete Subject:

> The tall boy in the classroom

---

### Mistake 5 — Confusing Compound Subject with Compound Predicate

> Ravi and Arun played and laughed.

Compound Subject:

> Ravi and Arun

Compound Predicate:

> played and laughed

---

### Mistake 6 — Ignoring "As Well As"

> Ravi, as well as his friends, is coming.

Main subject:

> Ravi

---

### Mistake 7 — Misreading "The Number Of"

> The number of students is increasing.

Subject:

> number

---

### Mistake 8 — Misreading "A Number Of"

> A number of students are absent.

Meaning:

> many students

---

### Mistake 9 — Assuming Imperatives Have No Subject

> Open the door.

Subject is understood:

> you

---

### Mistake 10 — Ignoring Meaning in Collective Nouns

A collective noun can be singular or plural depending on whether the group is treated as a unit or individuals, especially across different varieties of English.

# 55. Shortcuts

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 1 — Who/What Test**
>
> Find the verb and ask:
>
> **Who/What performs or experiences the action?**
>
> That is usually the subject.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 2 — Strip the Extras**
>
> Temporarily remove:
>
> - prepositional phrases
> - descriptive phrases
> - relative clauses
>
> Then identify the core subject.

Example:

> The box of chocolates on the table is heavy.

Strip:

> of chocolates

> on the table

Core:

> The box is heavy.

Subject:

> box

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 3 — Verb Agreement Test**
>
> If unsure about the subject, change the sentence mentally to singular/plural and see which noun controls the verb.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 4 — Number Patterns**
>
> Memorize:
>
> `one of → singular`
>
> `a number of → plural`
>
> `the number of → singular`

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 5 — Ignore Distractors**
>
> Expressions such as:
>
> `as well as`
>
> `along with`
>
> `together with`
>
> `in addition to`
>
> normally do not change the number of the main subject.

# 58. Final Exam Strategy

When asked to identify the subject and predicate:

### Step 1

Find the main verb.

### Step 2

Ask:

> **Who or what is connected to the verb?**

That gives the subject.

### Step 3

Everything that tells us what happens to/about the subject belongs to the predicate.

### Step 4

If the sentence is long, remove distracting phrases.

### Step 5

Check for:

- `of`
- `along with`
- `as well as`
- `one of`
- `a number of`
- `the number of`
- `there is/are`
- `either...or`
- `neither...nor`
- relative clauses

### Step 6

For error detection, check whether the verb agrees with the **actual subject**, not with a nearby noun.

> [!important]
> The fastest method:
>
> **FIND VERB → ASK WHO/WHAT → IDENTIFY SUBJECT → REST IS PREDICATE → CHECK AGREEMENT**

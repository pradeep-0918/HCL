# Conjunction

## 1. Core Concept

> [!summary]
> A **conjunction** is a word that connects words, phrases, clauses, or sentences.

Common conjunctions:

`and, but, or, nor, so, yet, because, although, if, unless, while, since, either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also`

### Intuition

Think of a conjunction as a **bridge**.

It connects two ideas.

Example:

> Rahul **and** Ravi are friends.

`and` connects two nouns.

> I wanted to go, **but** it was raining.

`but` connects two ideas.

> [!important]
> Quick test: If a word **joins two words, phrases, or clauses**, think **CONJUNCTION**.

---

# 2. Basic Meaning

Conjunctions make sentences smoother by connecting related ideas.

### Without Conjunction

> Ravi studied hard. Ravi passed the exam.

### With Conjunction

> Ravi studied hard **and** passed the exam.

The conjunction `and` connects the two ideas.

### What Can Conjunctions Connect?

A conjunction can connect:

- Words
- Phrases
- Clauses
- Complete ideas

Example:

> Ravi **and** Arun

Words

> in the morning **and** in the evening

Phrases

> I stayed home **because** it was raining.

Clauses

---

# 3. Main Formula

$$
\text{Conjunction} = \text{Connector}
$$

Common structure:

$$
\text{Idea A} + \text{Conjunction} + \text{Idea B}
$$

Example:

> She studied **and** passed.

Another structure:

$$
\text{Main Clause} + \text{Subordinating Conjunction} + \text{Dependent Clause}
$$

Example:

> I stayed home **because** it was raining.

---

# 4. Main Types of Conjunctions

There are three major categories:

1. Coordinating Conjunctions
2. Subordinating Conjunctions
3. Correlative Conjunctions

| Type | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Coordinating | Joins equal elements | and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet |
| Subordinating | Joins dependent clause to main clause | because, although, if, unless, while |
| Correlative | Works in pairs | either...or, neither...nor, both...and |

---

# 5. Coordinating Conjunctions

Coordinating conjunctions connect elements of equal grammatical importance.

The seven common coordinating conjunctions can be remembered using:

$$
\boxed{\text{FANBOYS}}
$$

| Letter | Conjunction | Main Idea |
|---|---|---|
| F | for | reason |
| A | and | addition |
| N | nor | negative alternative |
| B | but | contrast |
| O | or | choice |
| Y | yet | contrast |
| S | so | result |

---

## 5.1 And

Shows addition.

Example:

> Ravi **and** Arun are friends.

> She studied **and** passed.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If the meaning is **addition**, think **AND**.

---

## 5.2 But

Shows contrast.

Example:

> He is poor **but** honest.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If two ideas contrast, think **BUT**.

---

## 5.3 Or

Shows choice or alternative.

Example:

> Would you like tea **or** coffee?

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If the question gives a **choice**, think **OR**.

---

## 5.4 Nor

Connects negative alternatives.

Example:

> He neither called **nor** messaged me.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> `nor` commonly appears with `neither`.

---

## 5.5 So

Shows result or consequence.

Example:

> It was raining, **so** we stayed home.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If the second idea is a **result**, think **SO**.

---

## 5.6 Yet

Shows contrast or unexpected result.

Example:

> He is poor, **yet** he is happy.

`yet` is similar to `but` in many contexts.

---

## 5.7 For

Can introduce a reason in formal usage.

Example:

> I stayed home, **for** I was sick.

In modern everyday English, `because` is usually more natural.

---

# 6. Subordinating Conjunctions

A subordinating conjunction connects a **dependent clause** to an **independent clause**.

Common examples:

- because
- although
- though
- if
- unless
- when
- while
- before
- after
- since
- until
- whereas
- as
- even though

### Example

> I stayed home **because** it was raining.

- I stayed home → independent clause
- because → subordinating conjunction
- it was raining → dependent clause

---

# 7. Important Subordinating Conjunctions

## Because

Shows reason.

Example:

> I stayed home **because** I was sick.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Reason} \rightarrow \text{because}
$$

---

## Although / Though

Shows contrast.

Example:

> **Although** he was tired, he continued working.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Unexpected Contrast} \rightarrow \text{although/though}
$$

---

## If

Shows condition.

Example:

> **If** you study, you will pass.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Condition} \rightarrow \text{if}
$$

---

## Unless

Means approximately **if not**.

Example:

> You will fail **unless** you study.

Equivalent meaning:

> You will fail **if you do not study**.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> `unless` = `if...not` in many condition statements.

---

## When

Shows time.

Example:

> Call me **when** you arrive.

---

## While

Can show simultaneous action or contrast.

Example:

> She cooked **while** I studied.

> **While** he is intelligent, he is careless.

---

## Before / After

Show sequence in time.

Example:

> Wash your hands **before** eating.

> We went home **after** the class ended.

---

# 8. Correlative Conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions work in pairs.

Important pairs:

- either...or
- neither...nor
- both...and
- not only...but also
- whether...or

### Example

> **Either** Ravi **or** Arun will come.

> **Both** Ravi **and** Arun are students.

> **Neither** Ravi **nor** Arun came.

> She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.

> [!important]
> Correlative conjunctions must normally be used as **matching pairs**.

---

# 9. Either...Or

Shows one of two alternatives.

Example:

> **Either** Ravi **or** Arun will attend.

Meaning:

> One of them will attend.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Choice between two} \rightarrow \text{either...or}
$$

---

# 10. Neither...Nor

Shows two negative alternatives.

Example:

> **Neither** Ravi **nor** Arun attended.

Meaning:

> Ravi did not attend and Arun did not attend.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Two negative alternatives} \rightarrow \text{neither...nor}
$$

---

# 11. Both...And

Shows two positive elements together.

Example:

> **Both** Ravi **and** Arun are students.

### Recognition

$$
\text{Two positive elements} \rightarrow \text{both...and}
$$

---

# 12. Not Only...But Also

Adds emphasis to two qualities/actions.

Example:

> She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.

### Recognition

> [!important]
> If the sentence emphasizes **two positive qualities/actions**, think:
>
> `not only...but also`

---

# 13. Parallelism with Conjunctions

When a conjunction connects two elements, they should generally have the same grammatical form.

### Correct

> She likes **reading and writing**.

Both are `-ing` forms.

### Incorrect

> She likes **reading and to write**.

### Correct

> He wants **to study and to work**.

Or:

> He wants **to study and work**.

> [!important]
> Conjunctions often test **parallel structure**.

---

# 14. Subject-Verb Agreement with Correlative Conjunctions

This is a high-value exam pattern.

With:

> either...or

> neither...nor

> not only...but also

the verb generally agrees with the subject **closer to the verb**.

### Example

> Either Ravi or Arun **is** coming.

Both are singular.

### Example

> Either Ravi or the students **are** coming.

The nearer subject is `students`.

Therefore:

> are

### Formula

$$
\boxed{\text{Either/Neither} + A + \text{or/nor} + B + \text{Verb}}
$$

The verb generally agrees with **B**, the nearer subject.

## Example 11 — Subject-Verb Agreement

### Question

Choose the correct sentence:

A. Neither Ravi nor his friends is ready.  
B. Neither Ravi nor his friends are ready.

### Pattern

With `neither...nor`, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject.

The nearer subject is `friends`, which is plural.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{B. Neither Ravi nor his friends are ready.}}
$$

---

## Example 12 — Parallelism

### Question

Choose the correct sentence:

A. She likes reading and to write.  
B. She likes reading and writing.

### Pattern

The conjunction connects two activities.

Use the same grammatical form.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{B. She likes reading and writing.}}
$$

---

## Example 13 — Because vs So

### Question

Choose the correct sentence:

A. Because he was sick, so he stayed home.  
B. Because he was sick, he stayed home.

### Pattern

`because` already introduces the reason.

Do not normally use `so` together with it in the same simple cause-result construction.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{B. Because he was sick, he stayed home.}}
$$

---

## Example 14 — Although vs But

### Question

Choose the correct sentence:

A. Although he was tired, but he continued working.  
B. Although he was tired, he continued working.

### Pattern

`although` already expresses the contrast.

Do not normally add `but` in the same construction.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{B. Although he was tired, he continued working.}}
$$

---

## Example 15 — Exam-Style Error Detection

### Question

Find the error:

> Either the manager or the employees is responsible.

### Pattern

The nearer subject is `employees`, which is plural.

Therefore, use:

> are

### Correct Sentence

> Either the manager or the employees **are** responsible.

### Answer

$$
\boxed{\text{is} \rightarrow \text{are}}
$$

# 17. Shortcuts

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 1: FANBOYS**
>
> Memorize:
>
> `For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So`
>
> These are the major coordinating conjunctions.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 2: Meaning Test**
>
> Addition → **and**
>
> Contrast → **but/yet**
>
> Choice → **or**
>
> Negative alternative → **nor**
>
> Reason → **because**
>
> Result → **so**
>
> Condition → **if**
>
> Negative condition → **unless**

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 3: Correlative Pairs**
>
> Memorize these as fixed pairs:
>
> `either...or`
>
> `neither...nor`
>
> `both...and`
>
> `not only...but also`
>
> `whether...or`

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 4: Either/Neither Agreement**
>
> For:
>
> `either...or`
>
> `neither...nor`
>
> check the subject nearest to the verb.

Example:

> Neither the teacher nor the students **are** ready.

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 5: Avoid Double Connectors**
>
> Avoid these common combinations:
>
> `Although...but` ❌
>
> `Because...so` ❌
>
> Use:
>
> `Although...` ✔
>
> `Because...` ✔

---

> [!tip]
> **Shortcut 6: Parallelism**
>
> When conjunctions connect two elements, make their grammatical forms match.
>
> `reading and writing` ✔
>
> `to read and to write` ✔

# 18. Recognition Tricks

## Pattern 1 — Addition

> [!important]
> If the second element is simply added to the first, think **AND**.

Example:

> Ravi and Arun

---

## Pattern 2 — Contrast

> [!important]
> If two ideas oppose or contrast with each other, think **BUT / YET / ALTHOUGH**.

Example:

> He is poor but honest.

---

## Pattern 3 — Choice

> [!important]
> If the sentence gives alternatives, think **OR**.

Example:

> Tea or coffee?

---

## Pattern 4 — Reason

> [!important]
> If one clause explains **why**, think **BECAUSE**.

Example:

> He stayed home because he was sick.

---

## Pattern 5 — Result

> [!important]
> If the second clause is the consequence, think **SO**.

Example:

> He was sick, so he stayed home.

---

## Pattern 6 — Condition

> [!important]
> If one event depends on another condition, think **IF**.

Example:

> If you work hard, you will succeed.

---

## Pattern 7 — Negative Condition

> [!important]
> If the meaning is **if not**, think **UNLESS**.

Example:

> You cannot pass unless you study.

---

## Pattern 8 — Two Alternatives

> [!important]
> If there are two alternatives, check for:
>
> `either...or`

---

## Pattern 9 — Two Negative Alternatives

> [!important]
> If both alternatives are negative, think:
>
> `neither...nor`

---

## Pattern 10 — Two Positive Elements

> [!important]
> If both elements are included positively, think:
>
> `both...and`

---

## Pattern 11 — Emphasis

> [!important]
> If the sentence emphasizes two qualities or actions, think:
>
> `not only...but also`

# 20. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Avoid These

### Mistake 1 — Although + But

Incorrect:

> Although he was tired, but he continued.

Correct:

> Although he was tired, he continued.

Or:

> He was tired, but he continued.

---

### Mistake 2 — Because + So

Incorrect:

> Because it was raining, so we stayed home.

Correct:

> Because it was raining, we stayed home.

Or:

> It was raining, so we stayed home.

---

### Mistake 3 — Either + Nor

Incorrect:

> Either Ravi nor Arun will come.

Correct:

> Either Ravi or Arun will come.

---

### Mistake 4 — Neither + Or

Incorrect:

> Neither Ravi or Arun came.

Correct:

> Neither Ravi nor Arun came.

---

### Mistake 5 — Both + Or

Incorrect:

> Both Ravi or Arun attended.

Correct:

> Both Ravi and Arun attended.

---

### Mistake 6 — Parallelism Error

Incorrect:

> She likes reading and to write.

Correct:

> She likes reading and writing.

---

### Mistake 7 — Subject-Verb Agreement

Incorrect:

> Neither the teacher nor the students is ready.

Correct:

> Neither the teacher nor the students are ready.

The nearer subject is `students`.

---

### Mistake 8 — Confusing Because and Because of

`because` is followed by a clause.

> We stayed home because **it was raining**.

`because of` is followed by a noun/noun phrase.

> We stayed home because of **the rain**.

# 22. Quick Revision

> [!summary] One-Minute Revision

### Conjunction

A word that connects words, phrases, clauses, or ideas.

### Three Main Types

1. **Coordinating**
2. **Subordinating**
3. **Correlative**

### FANBOYS

- For
- And
- Nor
- But
- Or
- Yet
- So

### High-Priority Meaning Rules

- Addition → `and`
- Contrast → `but/yet`
- Choice → `or`
- Reason → `because`
- Result → `so`
- Condition → `if`
- Negative condition → `unless`

### Correlative Pairs

- either...or
- neither...nor
- both...and
- not only...but also
- whether...or

### High-Priority Traps

- Although...but ❌
- Because...so ❌
- Either...nor ❌
- Neither...or ❌
- Both...or ❌

### Exam Strategy

1. Identify the two ideas being connected.
2. Determine their relationship.
3. Choose addition, contrast, choice, reason, result, or condition.
4. Check whether a correlative pair is required.
5. Check subject-verb agreement.
6. Check parallel structure.
7. Look for common double-conjunction errors.
